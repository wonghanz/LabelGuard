LabelGuard — what a nutrition label actually says, once every number is on the same basis

An open source Android app that reads the table off a food packet, moves every figure onto per 100 g, per serving and per whole packet, shows how much of each declared number the rounding rules actually license, and checks the words on the front against the conditions they have to meet. It never gives the food a score, and never tells you whether it is healthy.

The problem in one pair of rows

PacketWhat its table printsSugars, per 100 g
A22 g per 100 g22 g
B6.6 g per 30 g serving22 g

Both are telling the truth. Only one of them is easy to reach for, and the packs that print a per-serving column are not usually the ones that improve when it is converted. The official comparison cut-offs are written against 100 g and nothing else, so almost every "is that a lot?" call made in a shop is being made across that gap.

Four things it works out that a pack leaves to you

  • The shared basis. Type whichever column you have and every row is expressed per 100 g/ml, per your stated serving, and across the whole packet — plus what share of the bag that serving actually is.
  • The interval behind the figure. Declared values are rounded by rule. LabelGuard reads the quantum off how many decimals the pack committed to, so a row printed 0.28 is held to ±0.005 and a low row printed 0.4 is held to ±0.1.
  • What the front of the pack can prove. Low fat, sugar free, high in protein, low salt, low energy and the fibre claims are tested against their published conditions. "Reduced", "light", "natural" and "no added sugars" are reported as unsettled, with the reason.
  • Whether the table contradicts itself. Its kJ against its own kcal, under both the exact and the long-used 4.2 convention; and its fat, carbohydrate and protein against the declared energy.

Salt is where two official numbers disagree. The claim condition for "low sodium" is 0.1 g of sodium per 100 g, which the same rule writes as 0.25 g of salt; the widely printed front-of-pack scale calls anything at or under 0.3 g of salt low. Both are published and they are not the same line, so LabelGuard names the one it used instead of quietly picking the kinder number.

And a printed zero is not a zero. A value declared as 0 is allowed wherever the amount does not exceed the contains-no threshold: 0.5 g per 100 g for fat and for sugars, 0.1 g for saturates. The app prints the licensed interval and the rule behind it, every time a row reads 0.

Where it declines to answer

  • No score, and no rating out of anything. One number summed over a whole table is the thing that makes a label unreadable.
  • No "healthy" or "unhealthy" — those are statements about a person's whole diet and body.
  • No share of your needs. Reference intakes describe a 2,000 kcal / 8,400 kJ reference diet fixed for labelling, not a target for anybody.
  • No colour on the bands. Low and high are printed in the same grey on purpose: tinting them green and red is the health verdict this app refuses to make.
  • No ingredient list, allergens, additives, origin, BMI, weight-loss claims, and no cycle or period features. The table, and the words on the front, and nothing else.
  • No product database and no barcode lookup — which is the same fact that stops it learning what you buy.

Privacy, stated

  • Runtime permissions requested: none. Nothing is read from the device at all; every figure is typed by the person holding the packet.
  • INTERNET is declared only for the optional pass you configure. With no key entered, no request is ever made.
  • Analytics, crash reporting, ads, attribution SDKs: none. No history is kept, and the form is wiped when the app leaves the foreground.
  • Screenshots are not blocked — a reading is the sort of thing you would want to show somebody, and there is nothing sensitive behind it to protect.

Bring your own key, for two sentences

No default provider, no free tier routed through anyone, no key in the repository. Settings takes a base URL, a model name and a key, stored in this app's private DataStore with allowBackup=false. What goes out is the per-100 figures, their intervals and which claims stood — never a product name, never a shop, never a photo. The prompt forbids recommending the food, calling it healthy, or introducing a figure that was not in the message, and the answer is labelled on screen as generated text rather than part of the arithmetic. Point it at an ollama or llama.cpp endpoint on your own network and even the figures stay local.

Frequently asked

Can LabelGuard tell me if a food is healthy?

No, and it will not try. It applies published per-100 g comparison thresholds and reports where a figure sits against them, which is a claim about the packet. Whether a food is healthy depends on the rest of a diet and a body, neither of which this app knows or stores.

Why does LabelGuard convert everything to per 100 g?

Because the cut-offs are defined there and nowhere else, and a per-serving column can report the same true figure so it reads smaller. 6.6 g in a 30 g serving is 22 g per 100 g, which is where the high-sugars line of 22.5 g starts to come into view.

What does %RI on a food label mean?

Percentage of reference intake: a share of the amounts set for an average adult reference diet of 2,000 kcal (8,400 kJ), with 70 g fat, 20 g saturates, 90 g total sugars, 50 g protein and 6 g salt. A convention for comparing products on a shelf — not a target, and never measured against you.

Why does LabelGuard print a range instead of one number?

Because declared values are rounded by published rule: energy to whole kJ and kcal, and fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt to the nearest 0.1 g, except that 0.2 g may be used below 1.5 g per 100 g. The app reads the quantum off the decimals the pack actually printed.

Why does a printed 0 g of sugar not mean no sugar?

Because a declared zero means at or below the contains-no threshold, which for sugars is 0.5 g per 100 g. A zero on that row licenses anything up to 0.5.

What does on the line mean in LabelGuard?

That the interval this figure licenses crosses a cut-off, so it cannot honestly be banded. 22.5 g of sugars may hide 22.4 or 22.6, and the line is 22.5. Picking a side would be the app guessing where the law says a range is legitimate.

Can it scan a label photo?

No. No camera permission and no vision call in this build, so there is no recognition to be wrong about. You type what you can read — which is also the only way to be sure a digit was not misread by a model.

Can LabelGuard compare two products and pick the better one?

It puts them side by side on one basis, row by row, and stops there. A winner needs a goal and a goal needs a person. Where the intervals overlap once each pack's rounding is counted, the row says the two cannot be told apart — which is often the true answer.

Does LabelGuard work with US, UK or EU labels?

The normalisation, conversions, tolerances and cross-checks work on any of them; US per-serving panels move onto 100 g the same way. The bands and claim conditions are the published UK and EU sets, so a US label yields fewer findings. Anything it cannot match is reported unchecked, not as fine.

Will it store what I buy?

No. Nothing is written to disk — not the packet, not the reading, not a history — and the form clears when the app leaves the foreground. A record of what somebody eats is the sensitive part of this idea, so that is the part left out.

Do I need an account or an API key?

Neither. Every pass works offline in airplane mode. A key only unlocks the optional plain-words rewrite.

Build it

Requires JDK 17 and Android SDK platform 35. AGP 8.7.3, Kotlin 2.0.21, Compose BOM 2024.12.01. The debug APK is signed with the standard Android debug key.

git clone https://github.com/wonghanz/LabelGuard.git
cd LabelGuard
export ANDROID_HOME=/path/to/Android/Sdk
./gradlew --no-daemon :app:assembleDebug
